01 — Scoring

How we score your prompt

Every prompt is evaluated on four axes by Claude. The result is a 1–10 score with three plain-English verdicts: what works, what doesn't, and how to fix it.

1–3
Vague

A single sentence with no context. The model must guess at your intent, audience, format, and scope. Results are generic.

"Explain climate change."
4–6
Developing

Clear goal but missing key constraints. You know what you want but haven't told the model who it's for, in what format, or to what depth.

"Explain climate change for a school report."
7–9
Strong

Specific, contextual, well-structured. Role, audience, format, and scope are all defined. The model can focus on quality, not interpretation.

"You are a science teacher. Explain three causes of climate change to 14-year-olds in 3 short paragraphs with one real-world example each."
10
Exemplary

Every best practice applied: role, audience, format, constraints, reasoning chain, and examples. Leaves nothing to chance.

"You are an environmental educator speaking to high-school students (ages 15–17). Explain the three primary drivers of climate change (CO₂, deforestation, methane). Structure: one heading per driver, 2–3 sentences, one tangible local example. Avoid jargon. Aim for 200 words total."
02 — Anatomy

What an ideal prompt looks like

Six ingredients — mix and match the ones relevant to your task. You don't need all six every time, but each one you add narrows the gap between what you mean and what you get.

1
Define a role

"You are a senior software engineer reviewing code for a junior developer."
Gives the model a persona, expertise level, and tone in one sentence.

2
Provide context

Who is the audience? What's the background situation? What do they already know? What should the output be used for?

3
Specify output constraints

Format (bullet list, JSON, email), length (under 200 words), tone (formal, casual), sections (Intro → Steps → Summary).

4
Show an example

"Here's a sample of the style I want: [example]." One concrete before/after removes more ambiguity than a paragraph of instructions.

5
Break into steps

For complex tasks, number the sub-tasks explicitly. "Step 1: … Step 2: … Step 3: …" forces structured reasoning instead of a stream-of-consciousness response.

6
Ask it to think first

Prepend: "Before answering, think through the key factors and tradeoffs." This one phrase measurably improves accuracy on reasoning-heavy tasks.
